The first person to see a wound is usually someone at home

A pressure injury on a heel, or an ulcer on the sole of a diabetic foot, does not announce itself. It starts small, at home, and the people around it are a spouse, a daughter, a home-health aide. By the time it reaches a clinic it has often become the episode every plan knows: an admission, a surgery, sometimes an amputation. Medicare spent as much as $67 billion on wound care in 2019,5 and the expensive part is almost always the late part.

WoundEye changes where the first look happens. A member or caregiver photographs the wound on whatever mobile device they already own, marks the spot on a body map and answers a few plain questions. A licensed wound-care clinician reviews the photograph and writes the assessment, and that assessment becomes the record.

The member gets an answer in plain language the same day. The care manager sees the same record, with a photo timeline, a trend line and the clinician’s note when a wound is getting worse.

No clinic visit, no special camera, no kit. The wound gets seen while it is still small enough to be cheap.

How it works

Four steps from the kitchen table to a clinician-signed record.

  1. Capture

    The member or caregiver photographs the wound on a mobile device. Image metadata is stripped and the photo goes to secure storage.

  2. Locate

    Two taps on a clinical body map, region then zone, so the same site is tracked visit after visit.

  3. Assess

    A licensed wound-care clinician reviews the photograph and its location and assesses type, severity and stage.

  4. Respond

    The clinician completes the structured assessment, gives the member guidance and sets follow-up. The record shows who decided.

Mr. R.’s heel was the same wound on day 2. What changed was who saw it.

Mr. R. is 78, has diabetes, and went home four days after a hip repair. On his second day home, his daughter notices a dark purple patch on his right heel. It doesn’t hurt. Here is the same month told twice.

Without a first look

  1. Day 2The family decides to mention it at his two-week follow-up.
  2. Day 14The skin has opened. The office refers him to a wound clinic; the first slot is two weeks out.
  3. Day 30The clinic finds a deep, infected wound. He is admitted.
  4. AfterThe plan’s first record of the wound is a hospital claim. Nothing shows when it started or what it looked like.

With WoundEye

  1. Day 2His daughter photographs the heel. A clinician assesses it that day as a suspected deep pressure injury and flags his care manager.
  2. Day 3His physician orders the heel offloaded and home wound care.
  3. Day 30Weekly photos and measurements show the trend, so the care team knows whether the plan is working.
  4. AfterThe plan holds a dated, clinician-signed record from the first day, whatever care he goes on to need.

Mr. R. is an illustrative composite, not a real member and not a WoundEye result; outcomes are measured in pilot.

What the record does for a plan

An earlier first look
The wound is assessed the day someone at home notices it, where your highest-risk members already are: post-discharge and in rural counties.
Evidence for the right level of care
Dated photos and measurements show what the wound needs, in either direction, with a licensed clinician’s sign-off on every assessment.
A decision trail
Post-acute coverage decisions are under review by the Senate and the courts.10 The record, with an audit trail written by the database, shows what each one rested on.
