A note for Medicare Advantage plans

The wound that costs the most is the one nobody saw in time

Of the 10.5 million Medicare beneficiaries living with a chronic wound, almost none have a wound specialist in the house.1 WoundEye gives the person who is there a way to get a clinician’s eye on it the same day.

$22.5B

What Medicare spends on wound care in a year. Surgical complications and pressure injuries cost the most.1

16.4%

Share of Medicare beneficiaries with at least one chronic wound.1

35.2M

People in Medicare Advantage in 2026, 55 percent of those eligible.2

45%

Growth last year in chronic-condition special needs plans.2

Most chronic wounds are first noticed at home, by family

A pressure injury on a heel, or an ulcer on the sole of a diabetic foot, does not announce itself. It starts small, at home, and the people around it are a spouse, a daughter, a home-health aide. By the time it reaches a clinic it has often become the episode every plan knows: an admission, a surgery, sometimes an amputation. The expensive part is almost always the late part.

WoundEye changes where the first look happens. A member or caregiver photographs the wound on the mobile device they already own, marks the spot on a body map and answers a few plain questions. WoundAI offers a first read. A clinician confirms or corrects it, and that clinician’s assessment, not the model’s, becomes the record.

The member gets an answer in plain language the same day. The care manager sees the same record, with a photo timeline, a trend line and a flag when a wound is going the wrong way.

No clinic visit, no special camera, no kit. The wound gets seen while it is still small enough to be cheap.

Mr. R.’s heel was the same wound on day 2. What changed was who saw it.

Mr. R. is 78, has diabetes, and went home four days after a hip repair. On his second day home, his daughter notices a dark purple patch on his right heel. It doesn’t hurt. Here is the same month told twice.

Without a first look

  1. Day 2The family decides to mention it at his two-week follow-up.
  2. Day 14The skin has opened. The office refers him to a wound clinic; the first slot is two weeks out.
  3. Day 30The clinic finds a deep, infected wound. He is admitted.
  4. AfterThe plan’s first record of the wound is a hospital claim. Nothing shows when it started or what it looked like.

With WoundEye

  1. Day 2His daughter photographs the heel. A clinician assesses it that day as a suspected deep pressure injury and flags his care manager.
  2. Day 3His physician orders the heel offloaded and home wound care.
  3. Day 30Weekly photos and measurements show the trend, so the care team knows whether the plan is working.
  4. AfterThe plan holds a dated, clinician-signed record from the first day, whatever care he goes on to need.

Mr. R. is an illustrative composite, not a real member and not a WoundEye result; outcomes are measured in pilot.

What the record does for a plan

An earlier first look
The wound is assessed the day someone at home notices it, where your highest-risk members already are: post-discharge, in rural counties, in chronic-condition plans.
Evidence for the right level of care
Dated photos and measurements show what the wound needs, in either direction, with a clinician’s sign-off on every assessment.
A decision trail
